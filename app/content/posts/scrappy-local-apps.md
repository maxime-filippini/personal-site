Picture this, you're part of a small risk management team at a management company.
You're using a massive risk measurement tool to produce your risk measures, which
are then fed to some kind of dashboard. In large organizations, these dashboards
can be built ba dedicated BI teams, but for smaller companies, Excel is usually
the go-to.

Excel, thanks to VBA ("Visual Basic for Applications"), is what I would consider
a gateway drugs for people like me, those with no formal training in software
development, but with a love for technical problem solving and automation. Nothing
can come close to the feeling of automating a task that would have taken a day
of manual labor, into a couple of button presses. 

But as time goes on, VBA will stop cutting it for most of these people. They will
want to explore technologies that are closer to what we may refer to as "real
software development", like Python,

common for teams to build their own "applications" using Excel, given its
ubiquitous nature in our workspaces. Although Excel is approachable, the
experience needed to maintain these tools is generally concentrated within a single
person, let's call her **"the Programmer"**.
